# Causality-Assessment-


Introduction

Causality assessment is an important part of pharmacovigilance. It helps determine whether an adverse event is related to a medicine or occurred due to another reason.

It supports patient safety by helping healthcare professionals understand the possible relationship between a drug and an adverse event.

 Definition of Causality Assessment

Causality assessment is the process of evaluating the relationship between a suspected drug and an adverse event.

Example: If a patient develops an itchy rash after taking amoxicillin, causality assessment helps determine whether the rash may be related to the medicine.

Who Performs Causality Assessment?

Causality assessment may be performed by:

- Doctors and other healthcare professionals.
- Pharmacovigilance professionals.
- Clinical safety experts.
- Drug safety physicians and medical reviewers.

 Factors Associated with Causality Assessment

A. Drug-Related Factors

1. Labeledness: Whether the adverse event is mentioned in the approved product information.

2. Relatability: Whether the event is reasonably related to the suspected medicine.

3. Predisposing factors: Conditions that may increase the risk of an adverse event.

4. Concomitant medicines: Other medicines taken by the patient that may have contributed to the event.

B. Event-Related Factors

The type, nature, severity and characteristics of the adverse event are evaluated.

C. Patient-Related Factors

- Medical history.
- Past history of similar reactions.
- Existing diseases.
- Predisposing factors such as allergies.

D. Clinical Factors

1. Temporal relationship: Did the event occur after starting the medicine?

2. Dechallenge: Did the event improve after reducing or stopping the suspected medicine?

3. Rechallenge: Did the event return after restarting or increasing the medicine?

4. Response to corrective treatment: Did the event improve after treatment was given?

Methods of Causality Assessment

 WHO–UMC Causality Assessment Scale

The WHO–UMC method assesses the relationship between a suspected medicine and an adverse reaction.

Its categories are:

- Certain: The relationship is strongly supported by the evidence.

- Probable/Likely: The relationship is likely, and other explanations are less likely.

- Possible: The relationship is reasonable, but other causes may also explain the event.

- Unlikely: The relationship is doubtful.

- Conditional/Unclassified: More information is needed.

- Unassessable/Unclassifiable: The information is insufficient or cannot be assessed.

 Naranjo Algorithm

The Naranjo Algorithm is a questionnaire used to assess the possibility that a drug caused an adverse reaction.

It includes questions about:

- Previous reports of the reaction.
- Timing of the event.
- Improvement after stopping the drug.
- Recurrence after restarting the drug.
- Other possible causes.
- Objective evidence of the reaction.

Scoring:

- 9 or more: Definite
- 5–8: Probable
- 1–4: Possible
- 0 or less: Doubtful

6. Practical Case Study: Amoxicillin-Induced Rash

Case Description

On 1 June 2021, Raj started taking amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for a throat infection.

On 2 June, he developed itchy rashes over his body. On 3 June, the dose was reduced to 250 mg three times daily, and the rash disappeared on 4 June.

On 5 June, the dose was increased again to 500 mg three times daily because the infection worsened. On 6 June, generalized itchy rashes developed again.

Causality Assessment

- Suspected drug: Amoxicillin.
- Adverse event: Generalized itchy rash.
- Temporal relationship: The rash developed after starting the medicine.
- Dechallenge: The rash disappeared after the dose was reduced.
- Rechallenge: The rash returned after the dose was increased.
- Alternative causes: Further information is needed to assess other possible causes.


1. WHO–UMC Method: The relationship between amoxicillin and the rash is assessed as Probable/Likely, because the rash appeared after starting the medicine, improved after dose reduction and returned after the dose increased.

2. Naranjo Algorithm: The score breakdown is as follows:

- Previous reports of this reaction: +1
- Reaction occurred after drug administration: +2
- Reaction improved after dose reduction: +1
- Reaction returned after dose increase: +2
- Other possible causes not established: 0
- Placebo test not performed: 0
- Drug detected at toxic concentration: No information (0)
- Reaction changed with dose adjustment: +1
- Previous similar reaction: No information (0)
- Objective confirmation: No additional confirmation provided (0)

Total score: 7 — Probable ADR.

Conclusion: Both methods support a probable relationship between amoxicillin and the itchy rash. The Naranjo score is illustrative and should be confirmed using the complete clinical information.

7. Implied Causality

Implied causality means that a suspected relationship between a medicine and an adverse event is understood from the report, even when the reporter has not explicitly confirmed that the medicine caused the event.

For example, a patient reports a rash after taking a medicine, and the medicine is identified as the suspected drug. This does not automatically prove that the medicine caused the rash.

8. Difference Between AE, ADR, SAE and SUSAR

AE – Adverse Event| Any unwanted medical occurrence after using a medicine, whether related to the medicine or not.

ADR – Adverse Drug Reaction| A harmful and unintended response for which a causal relationship with the medicine is suspected.

SAE – Serious Adverse Event| An event resulting in death, a life-threatening condition, hospitalization or its prolongation, significant disability, a congenital anomaly, or another medically important condition.

SUSAR| A Suspected Unexpected Serious Adverse Reaction. It is suspected to be drug-related, serious and inconsistent with the applicable reference safety information.

 An AE does not automatically mean that the medicine caused the event. An SAE describes seriousness, while an ADR describes a suspected causal relationship. SUSAR requires the relevant seriousness, suspected causality and unexpectedness criteria.

 Causality Assessment of Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI)

Definition

An AEFI is any untoward medical occurrence following immunization. It does not necessarily mean that the vaccine caused the event.

Types of AEFI

Vaccine-related reaction

- Vaccine product-related reaction: Caused by the properties of the vaccine itself.
- Vaccine quality defect-related reaction: Associated with a quality problem in the vaccine product.

Vaccination-related reaction

- Immunization error-related reaction: Results from an error in vaccine preparation, handling, storage or administration.
- Immunization anxiety-related reaction: Occurs due to anxiety associated with vaccination, such as fainting.

Coincidental adverse event

An event that occurs after vaccination but is caused by another condition or an unrelated factor.

Five Principles of Causality Assessment of Vaccine Adverse Events

1. Strength of association: How strongly the evidence supports a relationship between vaccination and the event.

2. Consistency: Whether similar findings are observed repeatedly in different studies or settings.

3. Specificity: Whether the evidence supports a particular vaccine-event relationship.

4. Temporal relationship: Whether the event occurred within a biologically reasonable period after vaccination.

5. Biological plausibility: Whether the proposed relationship makes scientific and biological sense.

These principles help assess the evidence; no single principle proves causation by itself.

Four-Step Process for AEFI Causal Assessment

Step 1: Eligibility

Check whether the reported case meets the requirements for causal assessment and whether sufficient basic information is available.

Step 2: Checklist

Review the important details, including:

- Vaccine and vaccination dates.
- Date and type of adverse event.
- Medical history and other possible causes.
- Laboratory findings and supporting evidence.
- Relevant information about vaccine storage and administration.

Step 3: Algorithm

Use the standard AEFI causal assessment algorithm to evaluate the available evidence and identify the appropriate classification pathway.

Step 4: Classification

Based on the evidence and the algorithm, the event may be classified as:

- Consistent with a causal association to immunization: Evidence supports a vaccine product-related, vaccine quality defect-related, immunization error-related or immunization anxiety-related reaction.

- Indeterminate: The evidence is insufficient to establish a definite relationship, or the findings are conflicting.

- Inconsistent with a causal association to immunization (coincidental): Evidence supports another cause unrelated to the vaccine.

- Unclassifiable: Adequate information is not available to classify the case.

Additional information may be required before a final classification can be made.

Scope of Automation in Causality Assessment

Automation can help pharmacovigilance professionals by:

- Collecting and organizing case information.
- Checking dates and timelines.
- Identifying missing information.
- Supporting questionnaire-based assessments.
- Highlighting possible drug-event relationships.
- Generating reports and summaries.
- Helping identify patterns in large safety databases.

However, automated systems cannot always determine the true cause of an event. Medical review, clinical judgment and human oversight remain important.

Conclusion

Causality assessment is an essential activity in pharmacovigilance that helps evaluate the possible relationship between medicines and adverse events. Methods such as WHO–UMC and the Naranjo Algorithm support systematic assessment. For vaccine-related events, the AEFI process helps classify cases according to the available evidence. Proper causality assessment contributes to better drug safety monitoring and patient protection.
